There's a line from The Martian I've carried around for years. Mark Watney, stranded on Mars with nothing but a broken habitat and the will to keep going, says that at some point everything is going to go south on you. Everything. And you'll think, this is it, this is how I end. But then you pick yourself up and get to work. I used to hear that and feel something. I thought I understood it. I didn't. Understanding that line and actually living it are two completely different things, and this year made sure I knew the difference. Turns out that's what inspiration feels like when the story belongs to someone else. When it's yours, it feels like standing at the bottom of a rock face in the dark, running your hands along the wall, just trying to find the first place to put your foot.

Not long ago I had everything I had worked toward. I had a six figure offer, H-1B sponsorship, and a bowl of hot ramen in Downtown Boston to mark the moment. If you know what Boston winters feel like, and what it means to breathe again after forty something interviews across four brutal months, you'll know that bowl of ramen was its own kind of celebration. I had my own apartment in Ohio. My own couch, my own kitchen, my own silence at the end of the day. For someone who had spent years living in spaces that never really felt like his, that apartment was proof that the hard years had finally added up to something. And then, just when I started believing that, everything fell apart.

Here's something nobody tells you about being the only immigrant on a work visa at a startup that's running out of money. Your job isn't just a job. It's the one variable that everything else in your life depends on. So when the company hit a money crunch and investor meetings started going cold, I didn't need anyone to explain what was coming. There was one person in that office on visa sponsorship. There was mounting pressure on the budget. The math wasn't complicated. What I didn't expect was the performance review where they gave me two weeks to build one of the core features of the product from scratch, and quietly mentioned that my employment would be under review. Anyone who has been in that spot knows exactly what those words mean. It means build this or we have a reason to let you go. I went home that night and cried. Then I got up the next morning at seven thirty and started building. Two weeks later I had finished the feature and built the entire mobile version on top of it. I shipped everything on Friday, ready for Monday. It still ended on a Tuesday, because some decisions get made long before the work is done.

When it happened, my mind didn't go to the job or the money or the visa. It went somewhere quieter and more painful than all of that. I started going back through my life, through every person I'd known, every moment I could have done better, trying to figure out what I had done wrong to end up here. I have always tried to be there for people. I have always tried to do right by the people around me. And I genuinely could not make sense of how I had ended up in this situation. I still don't have a clean answer to that. What I do know is that this kind of loop isn't really about finding the truth. It's just what happens when you care deeply about people and suddenly find yourself completely alone with too much time to think. The world doesn't care how good your intentions are. It just moves forward, and you either move with it or you don't.

What came after was harder in its own way. More than ten companies called me back. I prepared as well as I could, showed up for every round, and gave everything I had each time. One company took me all the way through a full interview loop. The feedback at every stage was positive. I started, carefully and against my better judgment, to believe that this might be the one. Then the call came. They had decided not to move forward because they weren't comfortable hiring someone who had been laid off so recently. I had passed every round. I had done everything they asked. And the thing that disqualified me in the end was the situation I had come there trying to get out of. Some things break you slowly. That one didn't.

The hardest losses this year weren't the ones I could measure. My brother got married while I was here, and I made my peace with missing it the only way you can when it's a choice you made yourself, which is quietly and without dwelling on it. What was harder to swallow was knowing I was going to miss my closest friend's wedding too. She is someone who has shown up for me more times than I can count, in the kind of quiet and consistent way that you only really appreciate when everything else in your life is going wrong. Every time she texted asking if I had booked my tickets, I would look at my phone and not know what to say. Not because I didn't want to be there, but because I didn't have a good way to explain where I actually was. When I was in Jersey she FaceTimed me to show me her ring. I watched that video more times than I should admit. Not because I was sad, but because her happiness was the one thing that week that felt completely untouched by everything going on around me. It was warm and real and I needed it. I have made my peace with missing her wedding. But making peace with something and having it stop hurting are not the same thing, and I have only managed one of the two.

My parents came to visit and saw me as I was, and that was the hardest part of this entire year. My dad is not someone who says much when things are bad, but I could see it on his face. The way he looked at the grey Ohio sky, and then looked at me sitting quiet across the room, told me everything. He left on the sixth of May and after that it was just me and my mom. I know she hated staying back. She never said it, but I know her well enough to not need her to. Being far from home in a city that had not been kind to her son was hard for her in a way she kept to herself the entire time. We went to Boston for my graduation and I walked through streets I used to love feeling nothing like the person who had once loved them. We sat in Fenway under a sky so grey it felt like the city had given up pretending. New York was no different. All three of us moved through it carefully and without much joy. At the Staten Island Ferry I stood at the railing and looked out at the water and felt, maybe for the first time, what it actually means to be at the edge of yourself. That whole trip was supposed to mean something for all of us. It didn't, and we all knew it, and none of us said so.

I am closing my chapter in Cleveland in a few days. I don't know what comes next or how much time I have left to figure it out. What I keep coming back to is this: rock bottom is not a place you pass through. It's a place you build on. Andy Dufresne crawled through five hundred yards of filth and came out the other side not because he was brave but because he never stopped believing that something better was waiting. Hope is a good thing, maybe the best of things, and it builds up quietly while you're busy just trying to get through the day. You only notice it when you look back and realise you're still standing. Life breaks everyone eventually. The good ones, the brave ones, the ones who did everything right. The only ones it doesn't break are the ones it finishes entirely. There is no shame in being broken. You pick up what's left, you hold onto the things that still feel good, you remember who you are, and you keep going. I am still collecting the pieces. I am still in the game. And I have not forgotten who I am.
