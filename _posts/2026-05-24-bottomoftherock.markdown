There's a line from The Martian I've been carrying around for years, one I thought I understood until this year actually handed me the bill. Mark Watney, stranded on Mars with nothing but a broken habitat and the binary choice of whether to quit, says that at some point everything is going to go south on you, everything, and you'll think this is it, this is how I end, and you can either accept that or you can get to work. I used to hear that and feel something like fire. Turns out that's what inspiration feels like when the story belongs to someone else. When it's yours, it feels like standing at the bottom of a rock face in the dark, running your hands along the wall, just trying to find the first place to put your foot.

Not long ago I had everything I'd spent years working toward. I had a six figure offer, H-1B sponsorship, and a bowl of hot ramen in Downtown Boston to celebrate, which if you know Boston winters and what it means to finally exhale after forty something interviews across four brutal months, you'll understand was its own kind of ceremony. I had my own apartment in Ohio, my own couch, my own kitchen, my own silence at the end of every day. For someone who'd spent years living in spaces that never quite belonged to him, that apartment wasn't just somewhere to sleep. It was proof that the equation had finally solved for something real. And then, the way life tends to operate when you start believing in your own variables, the whole thing got reset.

Here's what nobody explains about being the only immigrant on a work visa at a cash-strapped startup. Your employment isn't just a job, it's the master variable that every other variable in your life depends on, and when you pull it out the whole function collapses. So when the company hit a money crunch and investor meetings started going cold, I didn't need anyone to explain the probability to me. There was one person in that office on visa sponsorship, mounting budget pressure, and a room full of people doing survival math, and I knew exactly where I landed in that calculation. What I didn't expect was the performance review where they handed me a two week deadline to build one of the core features of the product from scratch and mentioned carefully that my employment would be evaluated alongside everyone else's. Anyone who's been in that position knows what that sentence actually means. It means build this or we have a documented reason to let you go. I went home that night and cried, and then I got up at seven thirty the next morning and started building. Two weeks later I had finished the feature and built the entire mobile version on top of it. I shipped everything on Friday, ready for Monday. It still ended on a Tuesday, because some outputs are fixed regardless of how much input you pour in.

The moment it happened, my mind didn't go where I expected it to go. It didn't go to the job or the visa or the money. It went into a loop, the kind where you stop asking what went wrong and start asking what's wrong with you, going back through every person you've ever known, every moment you could have done better, trying to locate the original sin that explains all of this. I've always tried to show up for people, always tried to do right by everyone around me, and I genuinely could not understand how I'd ended up here. I still can't fully answer that. What I've slowly figured out is that the loop isn't really a search for truth, it's just what happens when someone who cares too much about people ends up with too much silence and not enough ground beneath his feet. The world doesn't run a regression on your intentions. It just moves, and you either move with it or you get left standing in the same spot asking questions that have no clean answers.

What came after was its own specific cruelty. More than ten companies called me back, I prepared obsessively, and I showed up for every round and gave everything I had. One company took me all the way through a full loop with positive feedback at every single stage, the kind of process where you start, against your better judgment, doing the math on what life looks like on the other side. Then the call came. They weren't moving forward because they weren't comfortable hiring someone who had been laid off so quickly. I want you to sit with that for a moment. I had cleared every single round and earned their confidence at every step, and the variable that finally disqualified me in the end was the wound itself. There are moments that don't need time to break you, and that one didn't.

The hardest losses this year weren't the ones I could put a number on. My brother got married while I was here, and I swallowed that the way you swallow a decision you made yourself but still weren't fully ready to live with, which is quietly and without making a thing of it. What hit differently was knowing I was going to miss my closest friend's wedding too, someone who has shown up for me in ways that never asked for anything in return, the kind of steady and uncomplicated presence you only fully understand the value of when everything else around you starts coming apart. Every time she texted asking if I had booked my tickets, I would sit with my phone and not know what to say, not because I didn't want to be there but because I had no clean way to explain the distance between who she thought she was inviting and who I actually was in that moment. When I was in Jersey she FaceTimed me to show me her ring, and I watched that video more times than made any rational sense, not out of sadness but because it was the one thing that entire week that felt completely untouched by everything falling apart around me, genuinely warm and whole and good, and I needed to sit inside that feeling for as long as I could before the rest of it came back in. I have made my peace with missing her wedding, but I want to be honest about something: making peace with a thing and having it stop mattering are two very different experiences, and I have only managed the first one.

My parents came to visit and saw me as I actually was, and that turned out to be harder than everything else combined. My dad is not someone who makes a big deal out of things but I could see it on his face, the way he looked at the grey Ohio sky and then looked at me sitting quiet and hollowed out, and I knew the weather was the least of what he didn't like about what he was seeing. He left on the sixth of May and after that it was just me and my mom, and I know with complete certainty that she hated staying back. She never said so, but I know her well enough to know that being far from home in a city that wasn't giving her son what he needed was its own quiet kind of suffering for her. We went to Boston for my graduation and I walked streets I used to love like a stranger who had borrowed someone else's memories of them. We sat in Fenway under a grey sky that felt like the city finally dropping the act. New York was the same, all three of us moving through it gently and without much joy, and at the Staten Island Ferry I stood at the railing looking out at the water and understood in a way I hope I never have to again how a person arrives at the very edge of themselves. That trip was supposed to mean something for all of us. It fell short of everything it was meant to be, and none of us said so out loud, and all of us felt it the entire time.

I'm closing my chapter in Cleveland in a few days and I genuinely don't know what comes next. What I keep returning to is something I didn't expect to find down here, which is that rock bottom isn't a transit stop. It's a foundation, and you don't pass through it so much as you slowly learn to build on top of it. Andy Dufresne crawled through five hundred yards of filth and came out the other side not because he was fearless but because he understood something about hope that most people only figure out when they have nothing left. Hope is a good thing, maybe the best of things, and it compounds quietly in the background while you're busy just trying to survive, and you only notice it when you look back and realise you're still standing. Life breaks everyone eventually, the good ones and the brave ones and the ones who did everything right, and the only ones it doesn't break are the ones it finishes entirely. There is no shame in being broken. You gather what's left, you hold onto the things that still feel warm, you remember who you were before the world got its hands on you, and every passing minute becomes another chance to turn it all around. I'm still collecting the pieces. I'm still in the game. And I haven't forgotten who I am.

~ Written from the bottom of the rock. May 2026.
