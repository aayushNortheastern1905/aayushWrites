Do you ever feel like you have been standing at the edge of the abyss for too long? Like it has just been a while since anything in your life felt good, not one part of it? I do, sometimes. I remember sitting in Fenway Park on a gloomy morning that happened to be my graduation day, the end of the most expensive journey I have ever taken, financially and mentally both. I remember wondering what lay ahead of me even while sitting there in a cap and gown. I remember spotting my parents in the stands, cheering hard for me while fighting off the cold Boston wind, about as Boston a scene as Boston has ever offered anyone.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/graduation.jpg" alt="Walking across the stage at Northeastern" style="width:60%; min-width:240px; height:auto;">

It is September now, which means summer is officially over in this country, and I have moved four times this year. I no longer think a good life is one without wreckage in it, just one where you keep walking through the wreckage anyway.

It started with a word moving through the office before anyone said it out loud. Layoff. Mid March, nothing official yet, just a change in the air. Then it became official in its own cruel shape. Three weeks to build something that mattered more than anything I had built before, or I would simply be included in the cut. I said yes, because saying no was never really an option. Through all of it, the Ghatpande family stood like a strong headwind against the fall, and I will not forget that kindness, whether it was them calling me into their home for Gudi Padwa or Ashwin showing up to drive me wherever I needed without being asked twice. I built the feature. I shipped it. I watched it go live. And I was let go anyway.

By the last week of May I was watching my mother board a flight back to India, and something in me that had been holding steady finally gave out. I sat on the floor of an apartment that no longer felt like mine and cried the way I used to as a boy, watching a rickshaw pull away. Some griefs do not age. They wait.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/mom_balcony.jpg" alt="My mother, before she flew back" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

That same week strangers came to take the things I had sold because I could not carry them anywhere else. The sofa first. Then the bed. I slept on the floor on a rugged blanket, and it took me back to my very first night in Ohio, off a flight from Boston with no mattress, just floor and nerve. It felt like every ending was quietly becoming a beginning again. There was a party too, at Ajay's, the last one before I left Westlake for good. Everyone around me had someone, a family a phone call away. I sat through a blessing that night and drove home in silence beside Ashwin, all time hits filling the space words usually take up. He is the reason the Strokes became the soundtrack to a year I did not choose.

Boston meant relying on other people's living rooms. Fifteen days at the first one, no questions asked, because that is simply the rule when you show up with nothing. Then another home, another kindness, mostly bagels and cheap deli chips, grateful in every direction while quietly running on empty. A final round interview at Basata Health fell through the day before it mattered, the role suddenly filled without warning. I hated myself that night for something I never controlled. My parents stopped hiding the worry in their voices, and nearly every call drifted toward the same suggestion, come home. They had earned the right to say it. Other relatives called too, some out of real concern, and I was grateful even when I did not have the energy to show it properly.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/parents_nyc.jpg" alt="My parents in New York" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

Someone fed me almost every night that month. I mean that plainly, someone would call, pull me out, and put food in front of me. Right before a close friend's wedding, another door shut somewhere else, and I wiped my face and stayed up anyway for every ritual and every video call, because there are people you show up for no matter what you are dragging behind you. Then a friend named Aditya gave me something I had not felt in weeks. Warm food. A bed instead of a floor. Someone checking on me instead of just tolerating me. I interviewed twice from his place, once for Sunny Day Fund and once for Sarvam, chasing each one the way a man chases a job with nothing but hope left on the balance sheet. For a moment I considered simply going home to India for good. I still do not know why I did not.

Before the next flight, I went back to Castle Island with a friend, the same spot I had visited on my very first day in this city, three years earlier. We walked the loop and I said goodbye to Boston again, the way you say goodbye to someone who has watched every version of you and stayed anyway. Boston summers are the toll you pay for surviving Boston winters, and I felt that fully, standing there.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/boston_sunset_plane.jpg" alt="A plane coming in over the water, Boston skyline behind it" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

Seattle gave me a full month at Prasad's. Some evenings were just a walk down to South Lake with a dollar seventy five chai from Mayuri, no agenda, just steam and quiet. Other days I would just roam, downtown, Pike Place, no destination, no interview to prep for, just me and a city that did not know or care what I was going through. Having fun felt almost illegal during those months, like I had not earned the right to enjoy anything while my life was still in this many pieces, but I let myself have it anyway, a little at a time. We had two or three real game nights, Catan maybe twice, and lost badly both times. Most nights were simpler than that, just the three of us in the living room, watching something as dumb as India's Got Latent, cheering for Samay Raina's comeback, or laughing at Tanmay Bhat meme reacts until it was late enough to sleep. It was not much. It was enough. People took the things I could no longer carry and never once made me feel small for handing them over. You learn who your people are during the moving, never during the standing still.

Here is what I keep circling back to. My happiness and my sadness run on a dependency graph. Neither compiles on its own. Both are only a function of whatever situation I am currently inside of, and I have not learned how to refactor that yet.

This whole stretch has taught me the sharpest lesson of my adult life. Hard times do not just reveal who is with you. They sort people into three categories, and every one is different. Friends I do not speak to daily checked in without being asked. One picked up my call a week before her own wedding. Some people I would have vouched for without hesitation never once asked how I was doing. Not out of cruelty, I think. Just distance wearing the costume of being busy.

I do not want this to sound like a complaint. Needing people is not weakness, even on the days it feels exactly like one.

Some nights I stand in front of a mirror and cannot hold my own gaze. What looks back feels like a loser, even though I know I have wronged no one this year. I have helped people who had no claim on my help. I have stayed decent toward people who gave me every reason not to. Gwen Stacy once told a room full of people that we are not defined by the moments we get knocked down, only by what we choose once we are back up. I think of her more than I would like to admit, usually while I am the only one standing in the room. I tell myself the cards are still being dealt. I have to believe that, or none of this holds together.

I am down south now, on a contract with an end date, still hunting for something steadier, still living inside a body I have not fed or moved properly in longer than I would like to admit. I am about to turn twenty seven with a complaint I cannot fully put into words. Why does simply asking for consistency from the people closest to me sometimes feel like asking for too much.

I already know how the birthday will unfold. Quiet. Maybe alone. I have mapped it out, a beach, a book, the ocean doing what oceans do whether or not anyone shows up to watch. Last year I promised Bappa I would arrive at this point a better version of myself. I am not certain I kept that promise. Rocky never needed to win every round, only to still be upright when the bell rang, and some days I think just still being here has to count as keeping it anyway.

Some days I picture myself standing in the middle of a field that is entirely mine to lose, no army at my back, unsure which direction even counts as forward, quietly trusting that somewhere just out of sight there is a charioteer who already knows how this battle ends and is only waiting for me to stop asking and start driving. I do not always feel him there. I keep the reins anyway.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/seattle/fortune.jpg" alt="A fortune that read, trust in the universe and good things will come your way this Friday" style="width:50%; min-width:220px; height:auto;">

I am not going to pretend this chapter has a bow on it. I am tired, underfed, overworked, and I still do not know how many more cities this needs before it settles into something that holds. But I have slept on floors in three states this year and gotten up every time. I have been fed by hands that owed me nothing. I have been let into homes I had no claim to. That has to mean something, even on the nights I cannot feel what it means.

Every ending really does loop back into some kind of beginning. My own circular list, still running, still refusing to end cleanly. Maybe that is not a flaw in the code. Maybe that is simply what a brand new day looks like from the inside. Unfinished, borrowed, unsure of its own ending, and somehow, still compiling.
