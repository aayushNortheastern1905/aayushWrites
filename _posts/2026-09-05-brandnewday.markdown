Do you ever feel like you've been standing at the edge of the abyss for too long? Like it's been a while since anything in your life has felt good, not one single part of it? I feel that sometimes.

I remember sitting in Fenway Park on a gray morning that turned out to be my graduation day. The end of the most expensive journey I've ever taken. Money wise. Mentally too. I sat there in a cap and gown and had no idea what came next. I looked up and saw my parents in the stands, freezing, still cheering for me anyway. That's Boston for you.

Growing up in Mumbai, I saw a lot of poverty up close. A lot of homelessness. Your brain finds a way to register it and then go numb to it, because it has to, because otherwise you'd never make it through a normal day in that city. Back in August of 2022, right before my own life took a hard turn, I went out one night in Mumbai and ended up talking to an American tourist, some ex Microsoft guy on a solo trip trying to find himself. He couldn't wrap his head around homelessness in Mumbai, so I explained it to him, and I told him to stay far away from Dharavi while he was at it. I was so sure of myself that night. Never once did it cross my mind that I'd be the one facing that exact situation a few years later, just on a different continent.

When I first got to America three years ago, this wasn't even on my radar. Then I saw a homeless man on Boston's Centre Street, and later another one sleeping under the Prudential, and something in me quietly recalibrated. My idea of America had been built on movies, the version where the streets are clean and the dream is just sitting there waiting for you to pick it up. Turns out a country can have both the dream and the person sleeping outside the mall that sells it. I didn't have a framework for that yet. I do now.

It's September now. Summer's over. I've moved four times this year. I used to think a good life meant no wreckage. Now I think it just means you keep walking through the wreckage anyway.

It started with a word going around the office before anyone said it out loud. Layoff. Mid March. Nothing official. Just a feeling in the air. Then it got official in its own ugly way. Three weeks to build something huge, or I'd be part of the cut. I said yes. Saying no was never really an option for someone like me. I shipped my deliverables early. I built out a whole mobile app on top of it, something called VoicePrep, still up if you want to go look for it. And I still got let go, over a sync task that apparently mattered more than everything else I'd done, with the visa sponsorship cost quietly doing the rest of the deciding. Nobody says that part out loud to a person who just moved across the country and signed a year long lease three months earlier. During those three weeks, the Ghatpande family showed up for me hard. I won't forget that. They invited me into their home for Gudi Padwa when I barely knew them. Ashwin drove me around whenever I needed it, no questions asked. I miss that job more than I expected to. Good people, that Cleveland office. That's the part I still carry with me.

I kept going.

Last week of May, I watched my mom get on a flight back to India. Something in me that had been holding on just gave out. I sat on the floor of an apartment that didn't feel like mine and cried like a kid. The way I used to cry outside my grandmother's house, watching a rickshaw carry my mom away. Some kinds of sad don't get old. They just wait for you.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/mom_balcony.jpg" alt="My mother, before she flew back" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

That same week, people kept showing up at my door to take things I had sold online, because I couldn't carry them with me anymore. The sofa first. Then the bed. The weekend before all this, I'd been at a dealership trying to lock down a Jetta, feeling like a person whose life was finally moving in a straight line. That plan didn't survive the month. I ended up sleeping on the floor on a rugged blanket instead. It took me right back to my first night in Ohio, off a flight with no mattress, just floor and nerves. Even Bruce Wayne climbed out of that well more than once. The second time never hurts less than the first. It just proves you still remember how. There was a party too, at Ajay's, the last one before I left Westlake for good. Everyone around me had someone. A group. A family. A person a phone call away. I sat through a blessing that night and drove home with Ashwin in silence, old songs filling the space where talking should have been. He's the reason the Strokes are stuck in my head this whole year.

I kept showing up anyway.

Musafir Hoon Yaaron kept playing in my head that whole day, the traveler with no home and no fixed address, and that's about the size of what I felt landing back in Boston with everything I owned split between two suitcases. No apartment waiting. No plan past the next two weeks. Just a city I used to live in, looking at me like it didn't recognize me either.

Boston meant other people's couches and spare rooms. Fifteen days at the first place, no questions asked, because that's the rule when you show up with nothing. Then another place. Another kindness. Mostly bagels and cheap chips, grateful the whole time and quietly running on fumes underneath it. A final round interview at Basata Health got cancelled the day before it mattered. Role suddenly filled. No warning. I hated myself that night for something I never controlled. My parents stopped hiding how worried they were. Every call turned into come home. They had every right to say it. Other relatives called too. A few genuinely worried. I was grateful even when I didn't have it in me to show it properly.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/parents_nyc.jpg" alt="My parents in New York" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

I didn't buy many meals that month. Someone always got there first. A call, a table, a plate in front of me, and never once a comment about it. That's a specific kind of decency and I clocked it every single time. Right before a close friend's wedding, another door shut somewhere else, and I wiped my face and stayed up anyway for every ritual and every video call, because some people you show up for no matter what you're carrying. Then a friend I had lived with before gave me a roof to stay under. Treated me like a person and not a situation to manage. I ran two interviews out of that room, both long shots, and I went at both like they were the only ones left on the board. For a second I thought about just going home to India for good. I still don't fully know why I didn't. If I'm being honest, the only reason I made it through those months in one piece is that a handful of people kept choosing to be good to me for no return on their investment. A friend of mine literally kept me in his house in Seattle for a month. That's not a small thing. I don't think I have a big enough thank you for it yet.

I kept going.

Before my next flight, I went back to Castle Island with a friend, the same place I visited on my very first day in this city, three years earlier. We walked the loop and I said goodbye to Boston again, the way you say goodbye to something that has seen every version of you and stuck around anyway. Boston summers are what you get for surviving Boston winters. I felt that, standing there.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/boston_sunset_plane.jpg" alt="A plane coming in over the water, Boston skyline behind it" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

Seattle gave me a month at Prasad's. Some evenings were just a walk down to South Lake with a dollar seventy five chai from Mayuri, nothing to do, just steam and quiet. Some days I'd just walk around downtown, Pike Place, no plan, no interview to prep for, just me and a city that had no idea what I was carrying. Having fun felt almost wrong those months, like I hadn't earned it yet, but I let myself have it in small pieces anyway. We had a couple of real game nights. Catan, maybe twice. Lost both times, badly. Most nights were simpler than that. Just the three of us on the couch, watching something as dumb as India's Got Latent, cheering for Samay Raina's comeback, laughing at Tanmay Bhat reacts until it got late. It wasn't much. It was enough. People took the things I couldn't carry anymore and never once made me feel small for handing them over. You find out who your people are while you're moving. Not while you're standing still.

Here's the part I keep coming back to. My happy and my sad are reading off the same shared state, and whichever one finishes writing last is the one that wins for the day. I still haven't figured out how to fix that.

This whole year taught me the hardest lesson I've learned as an adult. Hard times don't just show you who's with you. They sort people into three piles, and all three are different. I've noticed something specific too. Some people never reach out to me first, not once, but the moment I do reach out, they show up completely, no hesitation, no matter what they've got going on themselves. Friends I don't talk to every day checked on me without me asking. One picked up my call a week before her own wedding. And some people I would have vouched for without thinking twice never once asked how I was doing. Not out of anything mean, I think. Just distance dressed up as busy.

I don't want this to sound like a complaint. Needing people isn't weak. Not even on the days it feels like it is.

Some nights I stand in front of a mirror and can't look myself in the eye. ~~Nothing wrong, just tired.~~ What looks back feels like a loser. Even though I know I haven't wronged anyone this year. I've helped people who had no claim on my help. I've stayed decent to people who gave me every reason not to be. I keep coming back to that Gwen Stacy line, the one about how we're not defined by the moments that knock us down, only by what we choose once we're back on our feet. I think about it more than I'd like to admit. Usually while I'm the only one standing in the room. I tell myself the cards are still being dealt. I have to believe that, or none of this makes sense.

I kept going anyway.

I'm down south now, on a contract with an end date. Still job hunting. Still living in a body I haven't fed or moved properly in way too long. I want to get back in a gym so badly it's almost embarrassing, I just haven't figured out how to fit it into a life that currently runs paycheck to paycheck. I'm about to turn twenty seven with a complaint I can't fully put into words. Why does just asking the people closest to me to show up consistently sometimes feel like asking too much.

There's a specific flavor of lonely I've gotten to know this year, the kind where you realize you don't just want more friends around, you want one person whose day includes you by default and not by request. I watched a friend of mine make a long distance relationship work this whole year, watched her partner put in the exact, unglamorous effort that takes, and it looked less like romance and more like the hardest job either of them had. I'm not in any shape to ask that of someone right now, and I know it. You don't really get to build that from the floor of someone else's guest room. But I'd be lying if I said I never look at it and wonder what it takes to walk toward it instead of just surviving next to it.

I already know how the birthday's going to go. Quiet. Maybe alone. I've mapped it out. A beach. A book. The ocean doing its thing whether or not anyone's watching. Rocky never needed to win every round, just needed to still be standing when the bell rang, and some days I think just still being here counts for something too. Last year I promised Bappa I'd get here as a better version of myself. Instead I think he's watching the roughest version of me I've ever been. Some part of me wonders if this whole year has been him keeping me at arm's length from joy on purpose, like a lesson I haven't earned my way out of yet. I hope I'm wrong about that. I hope I get to show him otherwise before this year is out.

Some days I feel like Abhimanyu, stuck inside the chakravyuh, knowing just enough to get in but not enough to get back out. Other days I feel more like Arjuna, standing in the middle of a field that's mine to lose, not sure which way even counts as forward. There's a moment in the Gita where Krishna doesn't comfort Arjuna at all, he just tells him to pick up his weapon and fight, and I think about that line more than almost anything else these days. A friend named Sid played that role for me more than once this year, showing up like he already knew exactly which direction I needed pushing in, no hand holding, just pointing me back at the fight. I don't always feel a charioteer out there guiding this. I hold the reins anyway.

Victory has a way of defeating a man more completely than any loss ever could. Bane said that once, and I think it might be the most honest sentence about myself all year. I won the last fight I was in. Landed the job, made the move, thought I'd cracked something. And I let off the gas the second I thought I'd arrived, and it cost me everything I built the moment life asked something harder of me. Winning made me soft in exactly the spot I needed to stay sharp. That might be the real lesson buried in all of this, not the losing, but what I do the second I think I've won.

I'm not going to pretend this chapter wraps up clean. I'm tired. Underfed. Overworked. I still don't know how many more cities this needs before it settles into something real. But I've slept on floors in three states this year and gotten back up every single time. I've been fed by people who owed me nothing. I've been let into homes I had no right to be in. That has to count for something. Even on the nights I can't feel what it counts for.

I kept going. I kept showing up. Every time.

If nothing else, let this be the one thing I tell myself before I close this out. Don't let anyone talk you out of what you're still trying to build here. Not your parents on their worried calls. Not the people who went quiet when you needed them loudest. Not even yourself, on the nights the mirror doesn't cooperate.

~~I have this figured out.~~ I don't, not even close. I used to think a brand new day meant something ending so something else could begin. I don't think that anymore. I think it just means waking up and choosing to try again, even when nothing about yesterday got resolved. That's it. That's the whole secret, if there is one.
