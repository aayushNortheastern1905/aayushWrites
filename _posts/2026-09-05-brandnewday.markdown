Do you ever feel like you've been standing at the edge of the abyss for too long? Like it's been a while since anything in your life has felt good, not one single part of it? I feel that sometimes.

I remember sitting in Fenway Park on a gray morning that turned out to be my graduation day. The end of the most expensive journey I've ever taken. Money wise. Mentally too. I sat there in a cap and gown and had no idea what came next. I looked up and saw my parents in the stands, freezing, still cheering for me anyway. That's Boston for you.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/graduation.jpg" alt="Walking across the stage at Northeastern" style="width:60%; min-width:240px; height:auto;">

It's September now. Summer's over. I've moved four times this year. I used to think a good life meant no wreckage. Now I think it just means you keep walking through the wreckage anyway.

It started with a word going around the office before anyone said it out loud. Layoff. Mid March. Nothing official. Just a feeling in the air. Then it got official in its own ugly way. Three weeks to build something huge, or I'd be part of the cut. I said yes. Saying no was never really an option for someone like me. During those three weeks, the Ghatpande family showed up for me hard. I won't forget that. They invited me into their home for Gudi Padwa when I barely knew them. Ashwin drove me around whenever I needed it, no questions asked. I built the feature. I shipped it. I watched it go live. And I got let go anyway.

I kept going.

Last week of May, I watched my mom get on a flight back to India. Something in me that had been holding on just gave out. I sat on the floor of an apartment that didn't feel like mine and cried like a kid. The way I used to cry outside my grandmother's house, watching a rickshaw carry my mom away. Some kinds of sad don't get old. They just wait for you.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/mom_balcony.jpg" alt="My mother, before she flew back" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

That same week, people kept showing up at my door to take things I had sold online, because I couldn't carry them with me anymore. The sofa first. Then the bed. I ended up sleeping on the floor on a rugged blanket. It took me right back to my first night in Ohio, off a flight with no mattress, just floor and nerves. Bruce Wayne climbs out of that well more than once in The Dark Knight Rises, and the second climb never hurts less than the first one. It just proves he still remembers how. There was a party too, at Ajay's, the last one before I left Westlake for good. Everyone around me had someone. A group. A family. A person a phone call away. I sat through a blessing that night and drove home with Ashwin in silence, old songs filling the space where talking should have been. He's the reason the Strokes are stuck in my head this whole year.

I kept showing up anyway.

Boston meant other people's couches and spare rooms. Fifteen days at the first place, no questions asked, because that's the rule when you show up with nothing. Then another place. Another kindness. Mostly bagels and cheap chips, grateful the whole time and quietly running on fumes underneath it. A final round interview at Basata Health got cancelled the day before it mattered. Role suddenly filled. No warning. I hated myself that night for something I never controlled. My parents stopped hiding how worried they were. Every call turned into come home. They had every right to say it. Other relatives called too. A few genuinely worried. I was grateful even when I didn't have it in me to show it properly.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/parents_nyc.jpg" alt="My parents in New York" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

I didn't buy many meals that month. Someone always got there first. A call, a table, a plate in front of me, and never once a comment about it. That's a specific kind of decency and I clocked it every single time. Right before a close friend's wedding, another door shut somewhere else, and I wiped my face and stayed up anyway for every ritual and every video call, because some people you show up for no matter what you're carrying. Then a friend I had lived before gave me a roof to stay under. Treated me like a person and not a situation to manage. I ran two interviews out of that room, both long shots, and I went at both like they were the only ones left on the board. For a second I thought about just going home to India for good. I still don't fully know why I didn't.

I kept going.

Before my next flight, I went back to Castle Island with a friend, the same place I visited on my very first day in this city, three years earlier. We walked the loop and I said goodbye to Boston again, the way you say goodbye to something that has seen every version of you and stuck around anyway. Boston summers are what you get for surviving Boston winters. I felt that, standing there.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/boston_sunset_plane.jpg" alt="A plane coming in over the water, Boston skyline behind it" style="width:40%; min-width:200px; height:300px; object-fit:cover;">

Seattle gave me a month at Prasad's. Some evenings were just a walk down to South Lake with a dollar seventy five chai from Mayuri, nothing to do, just steam and quiet. Some days I'd just walk around downtown, Pike Place, no plan, no interview to prep for, just me and a city that had no idea what I was carrying. Having fun felt almost wrong those months, like I hadn't earned it yet, but I let myself have it in small pieces anyway. We had a couple of real game nights. Catan, maybe twice. Lost both times, badly. Most nights were simpler than that. Just the three of us on the couch, watching something as dumb as India's Got Latent, cheering for Samay Raina's comeback, laughing at Tanmay Bhat reacts until it got late. It wasn't much. It was enough. People took the things I couldn't carry anymore and never once made me feel small for handing them over. You find out who your people are while you're moving. Not while you're standing still.

Here's the part I keep coming back to. My happy and my sad are reading off the same shared state, and whichever one finishes writing last is the one that wins for the day. I still haven't figured out how to fix that.

This whole year taught me the hardest lesson I've learned as an adult. Hard times don't just show you who's with you. They sort people into three piles, and all three are different. Friends I don't talk to every day checked on me without me asking. One picked up my call a week before her own wedding. And some people I would have vouched for without thinking twice never once asked how I was doing. Not out of anything mean, I think. Just distance dressed up as busy.

I don't want this to sound like a complaint. Needing people isn't weak. Not even on the days it feels like it is.

Some nights I stand in front of a mirror and can't look myself in the eye. What looks back feels like a loser. Even though I know I haven't wronged anyone this year. I've helped people who had no claim on my help. I've stayed decent to people who gave me every reason not to be. There's a scene in The Amazing Spider-Man 2 where Gwen Stacy stands at her graduation podium and tells the whole room that we're not defined by the moments that knock us down, only by what we choose once we're back on our feet. I think about that more than I'd like to admit. Usually while I'm the only one standing in the room. I tell myself the cards are still being dealt. I have to believe that, or none of this makes sense.

I kept going anyway.

I'm down south now, on a contract with an end date. Still job hunting. Still living in a body I haven't fed or moved properly in way too long. I'm about to turn twenty seven with a complaint I can't fully put into words. Why does just asking the people closest to me to show up consistently sometimes feel like asking too much.

I already know how the birthday's going to go. Quiet. Maybe alone. I've mapped it out. A beach. A book. The ocean doing its thing whether or not anyone's watching. Last year I promised Bappa I'd get here as a better version of myself. I'm not sure I kept that promise. Rocky Balboa never needed to win every round. He just needed to still be standing when the bell rang. Some days I think just still being here counts as keeping it anyway.

Some days I feel like Abhimanyu, stuck inside the chakravyuh, knowing just enough to get in but not enough to get back out. Other days I feel more like Arjuna, standing in the middle of a field that's mine to lose, not sure which way even counts as forward, hoping there's a charioteer somewhere just out of view who already knows how this ends and is only waiting for me to stop asking questions and start driving. I don't always feel him there. I hold the reins anyway.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/seattle/fortune.jpg" alt="A fortune that read, trust in the universe and good things will come your way this Friday" style="width:50%; min-width:220px; height:auto;">

I'm not going to pretend this chapter wraps up clean. I'm tired. Underfed. Overworked. I still don't know how many more cities this needs before it settles into something real. But I've slept on floors in three states this year and gotten back up every single time. I've been fed by people who owed me nothing. I've been let into homes I had no right to be in. That has to count for something. Even on the nights I can't feel what it counts for.

I kept going. I kept showing up. Every time.

If nothing else, let this be the one thing I tell myself before I close this out. Don't let anyone talk you out of what you're still trying to build here. Not your parents on their worried calls. Not the people who went quiet when you needed them loudest. Not even yourself, on the nights the mirror doesn't cooperate.

My life these days feels like a circular linked list, the kind we used to draw on a whiteboard back in undergrad. The last node never points to null. It points right back to the first one, and the loop just keeps running. No crash. No clean exit. Just motion, circling back on itself, over and over. Maybe that's not a bug. Maybe that's just what a brand new day actually looks like from the inside. Messy. Borrowed. Never sure how it ends. Still, somehow, still running.
