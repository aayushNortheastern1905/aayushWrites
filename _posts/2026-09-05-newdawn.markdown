It was a cold, rainy September morning in 2009. I do not remember whether it was a Saturday or something else, and honestly, my ten year old self had better things to worry about than preserving the calendar for future generations. What I do remember is that I had completed ten years on planet Earth. Ten years. At that age, this felt like a fairly serious achievement. I had survived a decade, and I was now entering the second one with absolutely no idea what the hell I was doing.

I was lucky enough to be raised by parents who cared a little too much about their only child's birthday. My mother would wake up early, do my pooja (it was a special day for her too after all), pray for me, make sure I was dressed properly, and somehow turn September 20 into an event. I would go to school wearing a different dress instead of my uniform, which as a child felt like getting away with a minor crime. Unfortunately, my birthday usually arrived around term examinations, so there was never much of a celebration at school. I never really cared. My parents were doing the heavy lifting anyway.

September has always been special to me. Obviously, I turn a year older on the twentieth, which is a fairly convenient way for a month to earn importance, but there has always been something else about September that I cannot quite explain. It feels like the beginning of a new innings. The year has already happened, things have gone wrong, plans have failed, and a few things you were convinced would work have quietly gone to shit. Then September arrives and suddenly there is this strange feeling that maybe there is still enough time left to fix everything, four wickets down against Australia, scoreboard looking absolutely fucking terrible, Dravid and VVS still out there batting like nobody bothered telling them the match was already over.

Ganesh Chaturthi probably has something to do with it too. Ganpati has always been very close to my heart. When Bappa came home, the mood in our house changed. My parents were happier, the house became louder, there was more food, more people, more movement, and somehow everything felt lighter. As a child, I looked forward to it every year. It was as if the house itself knew that something was about to change. The slump would end. Things would turn around. At least for a little while.

So naturally, on my tenth birthday, I expected the universe to cooperate. My parents had promised me a Lego set. This was serious business.

My mother had apparently spent some time on the internet trying to find where we could buy one, which in 2009 was already a fairly heroic undertaking because our MTNL broadband connection seemed to operate on the principle that every webpage first needed approval from the Government of India. Somewhere in the depths of Yahoo Search, we found a website that apparently said there was a Lego store at the Taj Hotel in Mumbai.

So we went to the Taj. There was no Lego store.

Which was, in hindsight, a strange place to end up. This was 2009, only a year after the 26/11 attacks, and Mumbai was still carrying real scars from what had happened there. The Taj had been one of the primary targets, and a year on, it was still very much in the middle of putting itself back together, refusing to let the building mean what the attackers had wanted it to mean. And there we were, walking into this absurdly grand, still-healing hotel because some random HTML page on Yahoo had promised a ten year old a Lego set that did not exist.

The terrorists never actually managed to take the Taj down. Yahoo, somehow, managed to take my birthday down. I am aware those two things do not belong anywhere near the same sentence, but at ten years old, standing in that lobby with no Lego set, that was exactly the sentence my brain wrote anyway.

There was no Lego store.

That was my first meaningful lesson about misinformation on the internet. I just did not know to call it misinformation yet. I called it Yahoo lying to me. A few years later, I would write a fairly scathing email to Yahoo support about their search and browser experience, because apparently I had decided that providing unsolicited product feedback to a giant technology company was one of my life's callings. But that is a story for another birthday.

For now, there was a more immediate problem. There was a ten year old boy without a Lego set, and he was crying. My parents had a problem. So we got back into the car and decided to race across Mumbai.

And by race, I mean we drove at approximately thirty kilometers per hour, because the great engineering marvel that is the Western Express Highway had generously decided that this was the maximum speed at which the citizens of Mumbai deserved to experience transportation. We went from Fort to Worli, crossed Bandra, and eventually made our way toward Malad because apparently Hypercity was the next confirmed civilization where Lego might exist. Mumbai traffic had already perfected the art of making a few kilometer journey feel like an intergenerational family commitment. Every signal lasted long enough for you to reconsider your career, your marriage, and occasionally your decision to leave the house in the first place. My parents were also fasting because it was Navratri, so the situation was perfect. My parents were hungry, stuck in traffic, trying to find a Lego set, while their only child was crying in the back seat because Yahoo had committed a fraud against him. This was not a birthday anymore. This was urban disaster management.

We finally reached Hypercity. No Lego. I cried again.

At this point, my mother decided that the appropriate response was philosophy. She told me that sometimes the more you desire something, the more it slips away from you. She said it in Marathi, obviously, because apparently existential suffering sounds more profound when delivered by your mother in Marathi. I cried even harder. Because what kind of mother gives a ten year old Stoic philosophy on his birthday? I did not need a lesson in detachment. I needed the fucking Lego.

Eventually, after enough crying and enough parental negotiation, my parents bought me a Nerf gun. My mother was not particularly happy about this development. I was. So the birthday was saved.

Looking back, I find the entire thing strangely funny, because that ten year old had just been handed one of the first real lessons life would spend the next seventeen years repeating, he just did not know it yet. You can want something desperately. You can plan for it, search for it, cross half of Mumbai looking for it. You can make your fasting parents sit in traffic for hours because some Yahoo page swore a Lego store existed. You can do everything right and still not get to choose the ending. Sometimes you do not get the Lego. Sometimes all you get to keep is how badly you wanted it, and some version of you spends years afterward trying to figure out what to do with that.

I have had good birthdays. Really good ones. I remember one of my birthdays being celebrated at PVR with a screening of *Lage Raho Munna Bhai*. I was probably seven. I remember another in my third year of college when all of us went to some restaurant near our hostel in Pune. I do not even remember the name of the restaurant anymore. I barely remember what we ate. But I remember everyone being there.

That seems to be the part that stays.

As I grew older, I started watching friend circles shrink. People started choosing who would come to their birthdays. People got into relationships. People moved cities. People got jobs. People became busy. Someone who had once been around every day slowly became someone you spoke to once every few months. I never really understood that transition. Maybe I never wanted to. Maybe I never knew how to ask for what I wanted.

One of my best birthdays was in 2022. Everyone came. I genuinely enjoyed every part of that day, and something happened that I cannot explain here, but it made the birthday particularly special for me. I remember the feeling more than anything else.

That is what birthdays are really about, I think. Not the cake. Not the restaurant. Not even the gift. It is the feeling that your existence matters enough for someone else to stop whatever they are doing and say, "Today is your day."

And somewhere along the way, I stopped getting that feeling as often. Then I came to America.

I think loneliness is one of the most evil forces in human life. I also think it is necessary. Those two things can exist at the same time.

Ever since I came to the US, I remember going to friends' birthdays, spending money, drinking, laughing, doing whatever I was supposed to do, and then coming back home and crying. I am not going to lie about that. I genuinely did. I still remember my first birthday here, three years back, coming home and just breaking down, somewhere between depressed and homesick, not entirely sure which word even fit. I would go out and try to enjoy myself, but there was always this feeling underneath it that something was missing.

Back home, birthdays were easy. There were always people. We would drink, dance, eat, make fun of each other, and somehow the night would disappear. Nobody had to plan a group chat six weeks in advance to determine whether seven people were available for dinner. Someone just showed up.

America was different. Suddenly, everyone had their own lives. Everyone was spread across cities. Everyone had jobs, relationships, roommates, deadlines, visas, interviews and their own little emergencies.

Loneliness does something to your brain at an atomic level. It cracks you open.

I sometimes imagine life as a maze, except the walls are made of clear glass. You can see everything. You can see where you want to go. You can see people walking around you. You can see the exit. You can even see people you love standing on the other side of the wall. You just cannot reach them.

That is why I think loneliness is also a kind of power. It forces you to look at yourself without all the noise. It makes you understand things you would rather avoid. It gives you clarity, sometimes violently.

The problem is that clarity has a price. The question is how much are we willing to pay for it?

When a person grows and makes progress toward a goal, I think there is a natural tendency to become lonelier. Not because success automatically makes you lonely, but because not everyone around you is going to share your focus, your priorities or your ridiculous obsession with whatever you have decided matters. That part is okay.

What hurts is losing people you thought were yours.

I see people around me now. My close friends in Pune, my friends in Seattle, my friends in the Bay Area. I see them on their birthdays and they almost always seem to have someone around them.

During my last week in Seattle, I was living with a friend and it happened to be his birthday. His girlfriend was in India, but she called him and did everything she could to make his day special. We went to Rattlesnake Ledge. In the evening, his friends came over and celebrated with him. His roommate, who had only been living with him for a few months, had bought him a nice gift.

I was sitting there watching all of this and I remember thinking, damn. So this is what it feels like to be loved.

Not the dramatic movie version. Not some giant declaration in the rain. Just someone remembering. Someone planning something. Someone calling from another country. Someone buying you a gift because they thought about you. Someone deciding that your day deserves to be different. That realization hit me harder than I expected.

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/newdawn/birthday_card.jpg" alt="A hand drawn card with my name on it" style="width:45%; min-width:220px; height:340px; object-fit:cover; border-radius:6px;">

The next night I had a panic attack because I realized I could not imagine any of that happening for me on my birthday. And then my twenty seventh birthday came.

Nobody in the city where I was living knew it was my birthday. I was also too shy to tell anyone, because apparently I am perfectly comfortable telling the internet about my deepest insecurities but somehow cannot tell a person sitting next to me, "Hey, tomorrow is my birthday."

So I woke up and went to a mandir. Then I went to Topgolf alone. I had a drink. I spoke to a few friends. I took a bunch of calls. And then I came back home. I did nothing. I slept. Later, I started preparing for an interview.

That was my twenty seventh birthday. Yes, sir.

For a while, I felt like this was some kind of punishment. Like I had been in exile for a long time and nobody had bothered to tell me when my sentence would end.

I miss my parents. I miss the kind of love I had when I was a child, when my mother would wake up and make sure my birthday felt like a birthday. I miss having people whose presence I did not have to negotiate.

Maybe that is why seeing my friends with their partners affects me so much. When I see two people being there for each other, something inside me just melts.

I remember looking at one of my friends and his partner, who are both close to me, and thinking that after everything else, maybe this is what a person really needs. Someone who is there.

I have also come to an uncomfortable understanding about myself. I am probably far from being in love because I am not in love with myself right now. No, I am not.

I know I am capable of more. My work is going okay, but I know I am cut out for something bigger. I want to improve my physique. My diet is shit. My career is in a strange place. I am trying to search for a better job while also trying to manage everything else that is happening in my life. It is difficult.

Sometimes I feel completely out of fuel. But the foot still has to stay on the pedal.

I turned twenty seven and started asking myself what exactly I had earned by this age. What am I a product of? The answer is not particularly pretty.

By twenty seven, I had lost a relationship with the woman I loved. I had moved to another country and started from scratch. I struggled. I eventually got an internship and then a full time job. I got my first apartment. I lost my job. I lost the savings I had earned. I lost my will for a while.

I watched my parents make their first combined trip to the US and felt like I had failed them. I survived some of the deepest loneliness I have ever experienced. I lived like a homeless person in Boston and Seattle, moving between friends' houses because sometimes that was simply what life required. I gave more than twenty six interviews in four months, which is the most interviews I have ever given. And now I am in Florida, working on a contract while trying to find something better.

If you had shown this version of my life to me at twenty two, I probably would have assumed you were describing someone else's life.

Everyone around me seems to have either a partner or a good set of friends they can depend on. People have people. I do not always feel like I do.

I used to think loneliness was purely a weakness. Now I know it can be powerful. Sometimes when I want to think or write, I actually crave being alone. I crave the silence because it gives me room to understand myself. But after enough time, even a person who loves solitude starts wanting company. And I do.

I genuinely crave a partner.

I want someone in my life. I want to feel some form of love again. I want to be treated as special by someone. I am tired of having birthdays where I am the only person responsible for making the day feel like it matters.

And that is exactly where the ten year old version of me comes back.

Because the moment I started thinking about how badly I wanted someone in my life, I remembered the Lego. I remembered my mother telling me that sometimes the more you desire something, the more it slips away from you.

For a long time I have treated the universe like some kind of vending machine, and it is not that I never put in the work, I did, plenty of it, more than plenty some months. But somewhere in there I still expected effort to come with a receipt, like wanting something badly enough and working for it was supposed to guarantee I would actually get it. The universe does not seem to run on that kind of contract.

What it actually rewards, I think, is action, not intention. It does not care how badly you wanted to get fit if you never changed what you ate. It does not care how much you want a better career if you stop applying after the fifth rejection. It does not care how badly you want love if you are not becoming someone capable of giving it back. Intent is cheap. Action costs something, and you only find out how much once you are already paying it.

So my gift to myself for twenty eight will not be something I can buy. It will be a version of me I actually respect by the time September comes around again, built quietly, one uncomfortable choice at a time, whether or not anyone else is around to notice it happening. And if I find a partner somewhere along the way, beautiful. If I do not, I will still have done something for myself.

That is the plan.

Maybe that is what September has always meant to me. A reset. Not because everything suddenly becomes better, but because you decide to start again.

The ten year old me thought a new beginning meant getting the Lego set he wanted. The twenty seven year old me knows better.

Sometimes you do everything right and still do not get the Lego. Sometimes Yahoo lies. Sometimes the Western Express Highway gives you thirty kilometers per hour and expects you to call it infrastructure. Sometimes you move across the world and discover that loneliness travels remarkably well. Sometimes you lose a person you thought you would have forever. Sometimes you lose your job. Sometimes you lose your savings. Sometimes you lose your confidence. And sometimes you lose yourself for a little while.

But you can still start again.

Maybe that is where Ganpati Bappa comes back into all of this for me. His whole lesson is detachment, and I am terrible at it. I get attached to people, to jobs, to identities, to futures that have not even happened yet. We want something, and then we start living as if we already have it, and that is usually where the suffering begins. I do not know how much of this he has planned for me. I just know I am still learning to let go of the outcome and stay inside the work instead.

That might be the strange thing about life in general. The equation never really closes. You never get all the variables at once. There is no final answer sitting at the bottom of the page. You make a move, something shifts, a new variable shows up, and suddenly you are solving an entirely different problem. Maybe that is okay.

I turned twenty seven yesterday. I am lonely. I am ambitious. I am tired. I want love. I want purpose. I want to become better. I have no idea how long any of it will take. But I am still here. And maybe that is enough for now.

The scoreboard is ugly. Wickets are down. The opposition has had about as good a day as they could have asked for. But the innings is not over, not yet.

So we bat. We wait, but never passively, never helplessly, because waiting still means working, still means taking the shots, still means getting rejected and coming back up to try again.

And maybe one day, when I look back at this strange period of my life, I will realize that this was not the punishment I thought it was. Maybe it was the innings where I learned how to stay at the crease.

Maybe a new dawn was never the universe's to hand me. Maybe it was just me deciding to get up for it anyway.
